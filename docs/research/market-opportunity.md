# Fortune-telling iOS apps: market opportunity assessment

**Prepared for Jackie Ye · 9 September 2026**

**Recommendation: proceed to a small customer-validation exercise, but do not yet commit to building a general fortune-telling app.** The category has established interest and many paid products. This review did not uncover a convincing unserved feature bundle, or evidence that our own app could acquire and retain customers profitably.

A viable business would need a specific audience, a reason to choose us over an existing product, and an affordable way to reach that audience. Better-looking screens and access to AI would be insufficient on their own.

## Scope and confidence

This is a public-source market assessment covering **12 iOS competitors**, using Australian App Store listings where possible, official product/support pages, original population surveys and subscription-industry evidence. **Jackie confirmed the audience is English-speaking customers, with no particular launch country in mind.** Consider the US, UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand when validating customer access and pricing; this report does not establish which country offers the best economics. Australia may be a practical place to recruit early testers.

Tarot and Western astrology are the primary competitive benchmarks. Chinese, Korean, Indian and Turkish traditions remain possible niches when delivered well in English. A bilingual product is not a requirement. Australian price examples and the GST model are local illustrations, not international price recommendations.

- **Observed:** public features, published purchase catalogues, survey results and platform rules.
- **Inferred:** customer motivations, possible improvements and relative attractiveness for a small team.
- **Unproven:** willingness to switch to us, current competitor profits, our conversion, retention and customer acquisition cost.
- Apps were not installed or purchased. Advertised capabilities are not verified usability. Prices are public catalogue snapshots, not observed checkout offers.
- The latest relevant population surveys found were conducted in 2024–25. They are not measurements taken in September 2026.

Detailed source notes: [competitor audit](competitor-audit.md) and [demand and demographics](demand-demographics.md).

## 1. Is there demand?

### Meaningful participation, with a smaller habitual audience

Pew's US survey of **9,593 adults in October 2024**, published May 2025, found:

| Activity | Share of all US adults |
|---|---:|
| Consult astrology, tarot or a fortune teller at least annually | 30% |
| Consult at least monthly | 14% |
| Consult at least weekly | 6% |
| Consult astrology/horoscopes annually | 28% |
| Consult tarot annually | 11% |
| Consult a fortune teller annually | 6% |

Methods overlap, so their percentages cannot be added. Twenty per cent of all adults participated mainly for fun and 10% for helpful insights. This suggests two different customer needs: occasional entertainment and an ongoing reflective practice. Neither measure identifies paying app users. [Pew results](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/05/21/3-in-10-americans-consult-astrology-tarot-cards-or-fortune-tellers/), [methodology](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/05/21/astrology-tarot-cards-fortune-tellers-methodology/).

**Australia also has direct participation evidence:** 11% of Australian adults said they consult a fortune teller, horoscope or another way to see the future in a separate 2024 Pew survey, with n=2,021. This question has no annual reference period and different wording. It must not be compared directly with the US 30%. It also does not measure app spending. [Pew original country tables, Q31c, p. 42](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2025/05/PR_2025.05.06_spirituality-around-the-world_topline.pdf).

### Demand is established; a new boom and easy profits are not

Gallup's May 2025 US survey found 25% believed astrology. Its age-group estimates were similar, illustrating why interest should not be reduced to a universal “Gen Z trend”. Belief and usage are different measures. [Gallup original survey tables](https://news.gallup.com/file/poll/692774/Paranormal%20PDF_7%2018%2025.pdf).

There is historical evidence of app spending: Sensor Tower estimated **US$39.7 million** spent in the ten highest-earning US astrology apps in **2019**, across the mobile stores it measured. This is an old vendor estimate of consumer spending, not current market size or profit. It confirms monetisation has existed for years. [Original Sensor Tower analysis](https://sensortower.com/blog/astrology-apps-2019-revenue-downloads).

I did not find a dependable public **2026 AU/NZ paid iOS category total** or audited current profit figures for these competitors. Rolling app-analytics snapshots are documented separately with their limitations; they are not used to forecast our revenue. Generic billion-dollar market forecasts were not used.

**Commercial interpretation:** enough demand to test a proposition, but no basis yet for estimating our share of the market. Survey interest, downloads, subscriptions and profit are four different things.

These products compete with free horoscope websites, creator content, general AI chatbots, physical card decks and in-person readers as well as other apps. Customers do not have to switch from one app to another to meet the same need.

### Monetisation models and their practical trade-offs

| Model | Why customers pay | Implication for us |
|---|---|---|
| Subscription | Continuing personal content, lessons or a recurring practice | Attractive recurring revenue only if people return and renew. Requires ongoing value and content upkeep. |
| Reading/question credits or one-off reports | A particular question, annual reading or specific piece of content | Better fit for episodic use; harder to forecast and repeat sales are still needed. Clearly show the cost before each reading. |
| Human consultations | Attention and trust in a particular reader | Higher transaction values, but reader payment, recruitment, quality, availability and disputes make this a different operating business. |
| Advertising | Free casual entertainment | Needs reach and repeated impressions; can weaken the calm/private experience. |
| Decks, courses and related products | Ownership, learning or a creator relationship | Can support distribution and revenue; licensed content and physical fulfilment add work. |

Do not assume marketplace customer spending is platform revenue: the reader must be paid, and the platform's actual take rate was not established here.

## 3. Which types suit which audiences?

**Start with a person's existing practice, language and reason for using it. Demographics help recruitment; they do not define an individual's preferences.** No reliable evidence found here establishes which demographic pays the most for fortune-telling iOS apps.

In the large US Pew survey, younger adults were more likely to use tarot, and women and LGBT adults were more engaged with some practices. These are US usage patterns, not Australian purchase rates. Older adults should not be treated as uninterested; Gallup's astrology-belief estimates did not show a comparable age gradient. [Pew demographic analysis](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/05/21/3-in-10-americans-consult-astrology-tarot-cards-or-fortune-tellers/), [Gallup tables](https://news.gallup.com/file/poll/692774/Paranormal%20PDF_7%2018%2025.pdf).

The following matches are **product hypotheses to test**, not measured purchasing preferences:

| Audience to investigate | Suitable formats to test | Commercial assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Adults 18–24 seeking social entertainment | Short tarot readings, Western horoscope, opt-in friend/group activities | Easy to sample/share; a weekly subscription may be a poor fit for casual use. Budget sensitivity needs research. |
| Adults 25–44 who already maintain a reflective practice | Tarot/oracle practice, thoughtful astrology, saved personal reflections and learning | My first recruitment hypothesis for a recurring product. Existing behaviour is more important than this provisional age bracket. Strong competition. |
| Adults 45+ who already consult readers or have a card practice | Clear text/audio readings, familiar traditions, dependable archives or a known human reader | Test usability and payment directly; do not assume older customers want simpler beliefs, higher prices or human readers. |
| Existing Vedic/Jyotish users, in India or diaspora communities | Birth-chart interpretation, traditional terminology, matching and access to a trusted astrologer | Culturally specific research needed; Astrotalk already supplies much of this. Indian survey findings do not establish diaspora demand. [Pew India](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2021/06/29/religious-beliefs-2/). |
| English-speaking BaZi/Chinese astrology users or interested learners | One carefully explained tradition, plain-English definitions of traditional terms, clear calculation assumptions and reports | Potentially focused niche, but FateTell and E-Bazi already compete. Expert credibility and language quality would matter. [National Museum of Singapore on BaZi](https://www.roots.gov.sg/Collection-Landing/listing/1263437). |
| English-speaking customers interested in Saju | Accessible English explanations that preserve the tradition's meaning | A distinct content/localisation exercise; SaJu already exists. Do not equate all East Asian traditions. |
| Coffee-reading enthusiasts | Coffee ritual, imagery and shared storytelling | A culturally rooted social format, already served by Faladdin. [UNESCO on Turkish coffee culture](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/turkish-coffee-culture-and-tradition-00645). |
| Curious customers attracted to palmistry, numerology or dream interpretation | A short standalone experience or an optional additional mode | Insufficient evidence to rank these by age or payment. A camera effect alone offers little reason to retain an app. |

BaZi and Saju use birth information; tarot and question-based rituals can offer a useful first experience with less personal data. Human reading is a delivery format that can span several traditions.

For an initial business test, I would recruit **adults with recent repeat use or actual spending**, then deliberately include different ages and genders. I would not target minors as the initial paying audience.

## 4. What could we improve?

The review examples below generate testable questions. They are selected individual accounts, sometimes old, and do not establish the prevalence of a problem or that it persists today. Dates and sources are in the [detailed review audit](competitor-audit.md).

| Potential improvement | Evidence and competitive reality | How to demonstrate “better” |
|---|---|---|
| Clear price and paid entitlement | Selected Nebula and Pattern reviews describe billing or paid-area confusion. Nebula web billing is distinct from Apple billing. | A user can explain the total, renewal date and included features before paying; monitor disputes and refunds. |
| A focused, calm reading experience | A Pattern review described clutter/audio/navigation problems; a recent Co–Star reviewer disliked reduced specificity. | Observe whether users can get a relevant reading and find it again without help; compare directly with the incumbent. |
| Consistent personal context over time | Useful hypothesis, but journals, analytics, follow-up prompts and conversation already exist. | Users can identify which previous note informed an interpretation, edit remembered information and say why this is better than their current tool. |
| Stronger cultural explanation | Existing niche tools already offer AI and multiple systems. Language and interpretation quality remain untested here. | Review with practitioners and native speakers; show why a calculation or interpretation was produced and acknowledge alternative conventions. |
| Dependable local timing and saved work | An old CHANI timezone complaint suggests an AU test case, not a proven present gap. | Check local-day boundaries, daylight saving, access to history, restore purchases and export. These are quality requirements, not a moat. |
| A voice and programme customers trust | CHANI already demonstrates human-authored positioning; “no AI” or “AI” alone is not novel. | Users prefer the actual writing/teaching and return for the next instalment at the stated price. |

A promise of more accurate predictions would not be a credible product advantage. We can assess consistency, faithful implementation of a tradition, writing quality and user experience. This research does not establish that these systems reliably predict future events.

AI could help interpret a question or explain a precomputed result, but would need controlled costs, disclosed data use, editable memory and checks for invented facts. A named model or paid API has not been selected. Clear rules should handle calculations where possible; generative text should not quietly invent the underlying chart.

## 5. Where might a small team have an opportunity?

### Three credible directions

| Direction | Potential advantage | Main difficulty | My assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A focused tarot practice developed with a named teacher or creator** | Specific teaching style, a defined learning programme and a reachable community. Start with one practice/customer need. | Labyrinthos already covers most generic features. Requires a real content/distribution partnership and a demonstrably preferred experience. | **First hypothesis to test**, because a sample programme is cheap to assess before an app is built. No partner or gap is assumed to exist. |
| **An explainable Chinese astrology product in English** | Clear explanations and one tradition handled well for a precisely chosen English-speaking audience. | FateTell and E-Bazi already offer modern/AI experiences. Requires practitioner expertise, terminology and date/time correctness. | Secondary hypothesis, worth comparing if we have access to relevant expertise and customers. |
| **A broad AI fortune app or new human-reader marketplace** | Large apparent feature coverage or higher-value transactions. | Crowded substitutes; generic AI is easily copied. Human marketplaces add substantial supply and service operations. | **Do not prioritise** without an unusual distribution or practitioner advantage. |

These are hypotheses about execution and customer access, not discoveries of empty markets. The first direction is a cheaper **validation starting point**, not a commitment to tarot or a conclusion that it has the highest profit potential.

An initial concept would only become a build recommendation after customers name a recurring unmet need and prefer a concrete solution to the strongest existing competitor. Aim to be better for one identifiable customer, rather than better across every tradition and feature.

### What would make the advantage durable?

The strongest candidates are an audience we can reach repeatedly, distinctive licensed/expert content, and a product people voluntarily return to. A creator relationship can reduce advertising dependence, but brings revenue-share costs and reliance on that creator. Private saved history can add usefulness over time; users should retain export and deletion rather than being trapped.

Transparent billing and careful privacy are good foundations. They are not, by themselves, proven reasons people will switch or pay.

## 6. Could it make worthwhile money?

### Broad app benchmarks caution against easy assumptions

RevenueCat's 2026 study covers over 115,000 subscription apps and more than US$16 billion of revenue. Across its dataset, median day-35 download-to-paid conversion was **2.1% for freemium apps**. At 12 months, monthly-plan subscriber retention was **6.1% for AI apps versus 9.5% for non-AI apps**. These are broad observed benchmarks, not fortune-telling-specific forecasts or proof that AI causes churn. [RevenueCat 2026](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps).

Conversion means the share of installers who become paying subscribers. Retention means how many continue; churn is the share lost. A polished first reading can sell an initial subscription while failing to provide a reason to renew.

### Illustrative Australian monthly model

**These are chosen assumptions, not competitor metrics, API quotes or a sales forecast.**

- Consumer subscription: **A$9.99/month**.
- Illustrative Australian tax treatment: price includes **10% GST**.
- Apple commission: **15%**, assuming enrolment and eligibility for its Small Business Program.
- Variable service cost: **A$0.50 per paying subscriber per month**, a placeholder to replace with measured delivery cost.
- Excludes advertising, free-user delivery costs, refunds, expert content, fixed expenses, founder time and income tax. Annual discounts would change the result.

Apple describes the reduced commission and its eligibility criteria, and explains that displayed prices include applicable taxes it collects and remits. Actual proceeds vary by territory and tax treatment. [Small Business Program](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/small-business-program/), [subscription proceeds and taxes](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/subscriptions/). [ATO GST explanation](https://www.ato.gov.au/businesses-and-organisations/international-tax-for-business/in-detail/trans-tasman-rules/comparing-the-new-zealand-and-australian-tax-system/goods-and-services-tax).

**Monthly contribution per subscriber = A$9.99 ÷ 1.10 × 85% − A$0.50 = A$7.22.**

“Contribution” is the amount left to pay advertising, fixed costs and ourselves. It is not profit.

| Paying subscribers | Monthly customer billings | Monthly contribution before the excluded costs |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 100 | A$999 | A$722 |
| 500 | A$4,995 | A$3,610 |
| 1,000 | A$9,990 | A$7,220 |

About **693 retained paying subscribers** produce A$5,000 of monthly contribution under these assumptions, before acquisition and the other excluded costs.

Now assume **3% of installs become payers** and paid acquisition costs **A$2 per install**. One payer costs roughly **A$66.67** to acquire. Merely replacing cancellations in a business with 1,000 paying subscribers gives:

| Monthly payer churn assumption | Replacement installs required/month | Replacement advertising/month | Contribution left after those ads, before other excluded costs |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 5% | 1,667 | A$3,333 | A$3,886 |
| 10% | 3,333 | A$6,667 | A$553 |
| 20% | 6,667 | A$13,333 | **−A$6,114** |

This models maintaining an established base, not the initial cost of building it. Five or ten per cent constant monthly churn would imply stronger year-long retention than the broad AI monthly-plan benchmark; they should not be treated as safe assumptions.

The practical lesson is that **retention and customer acquisition can matter more than the cost of generating readings**. Organic discovery may improve the numbers, but content production, partner shares and our time still cost something.

### Pricing to test, rather than decide now

For a recurring self-service product, test a meaningful free sample followed by a clearly stated **A$9.99 monthly offer**. For an episodic product, compare a transparent one-off reading or programme price. These are test proposals, not committed pricing.

Introduce an annual offer only after observing continuing value; upfront annual cash should not be mistaken for monthly profit or proof of renewal. A human-reader service would need a separate financial model.

## 7. A material iOS entry requirement

Apple's current **App Review Guideline 4.3(b)** explicitly names fortune telling as an established category and requires a meaningfully different or improved experience for new submissions. Acceptance is not guaranteed by a nicer interface, more modes or adding AI. The core improvement must be visible and explainable during review. [Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/#design).

For planning, assume digital readings and subscriptions use Apple's in-app purchases. Apple has a separate rule permitting other payment methods for qualifying real-time one-to-one services; it should not be assumed to cover asynchronous reports or AI chat. Recheck the actual service design before choosing payments. [Apple payment rules](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/#other-purchase-methods).

This strengthens the case for validating the distinctive workflow before investing in a full native app.

## 8. What I recommend doing next

**Run a focused validation round, then make a build/no-build decision.** No platform, service, partnership, public deployment or advertising purchase is being selected by this report.

1. **Interview 15–20 existing users in one reachable audience.** Prioritise people who have used a relevant product recently; include both payers and people who cancelled. Ask about their last real session, bill, frustration and current workaround. Observe the strongest incumbent's current free flow and AU paywall.
2. **Test two concrete concept samples against that incumbent.** Start with a creator-led tarot programme and compare a culturally specific concept only if expertise/customer access is available. Show an actual reading, repeat-use flow and price. Ask what it would replace.
3. **Run a small paid pilot before building the full app.** A suggested directional gate for 20 participants is at least 12 completing three sessions in a fortnight and at least five paying the proposed monthly price. These are chosen decision thresholds, not industry benchmarks. A warm, small cohort cannot establish market-wide conversion.
4. **Observe renewal and reasons for cancellation.** Seek actual second-month payments and continued use, not just positive feedback or a discounted annual purchase. At least three of the first five payers renewing would justify further investigation, not prove the economics.
5. **Replace every economic assumption with measurements.** Include acquisition effort, partner fees, free-user costs, refunds and founder time. Reassess Apple differentiation before substantial native development.
6. **Stop or revise the concept if the advantage does not survive comparison.** If people prefer existing free tools, return only when prompted, or cannot name a reason to pay us, more features are unlikely to solve the underlying problem.

The next useful deliverable is an interview guide and two concrete concept cards, rather than an app implementation plan. The central decision remains: **which existing customers have a recurring problem that we can solve noticeably better, and can we reach them at a sustainable cost?**
